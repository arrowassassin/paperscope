# Thin libraries win arguments, not installs

Four of the five candidate ideas do not survive contact with late-2026 market evidence, and the one that does only survives after being rebuilt as something other than a library. The thin typed provider-agnostic layer (A), the "SQLAlchemy-level" helper (E), and git-trackable prompt versioning (D) are falsified on differentiation: each is already served by a free, enormous or trivially reimplementable substitute, and the closest dedicated attempts sit at 0–3 GitHub stars. The Go/Rust retry helper (B) is falsified as a *retry* product but survives in a narrower form, because the Go team's own survey shows AI feature work **declining** in that ecosystem — 78% of respondents build no AI features, a 14-point shift away from 2024 ([Go Developer Survey 2025](https://go.dev/blog/survey2025)). The supply-chain idea (C) is the exception, and only because the 2026 evidence destroyed its original mechanism: malicious LiteLLM 1.107.0 images shipped with **a valid signature from the previous key**, so "hardened and signed" cannot be the claim ([LiteLLM incident report](https://docs.litellm.ai/blog/security-incident-oct-21-2026)). What survives is an auditor that recomputes evidence locally, ranked first at 22/25 against an MCP readiness audit at 21/25 and a Go failure-semantics client at 17/25. The deciding variable was never the size of the gap — almost every gap probed already has three to six zero-star attempts — but whether the pain has a budget holder, whether a 200-line script closes it, and whether an incumbent has publicly declined the territory.

## Four of five ideas die on the same three-part test

The drop test that organizes this report is simple: name the incumbent, name what it structurally cannot do, and name who pays. Missing any one of the three produces a two-star repository, and the measured graveyard confirms it — model-deprecation CI scanners exist as four separate projects with **0, 0, 0 and 3 stars** ([deprecation-desk](https://github.com/Ort0x36/deprecation-desk), [model-retire](https://github.com/BYDTechSolutions/model-retire)), LLM record-replay "VCR" libraries as at least seven ([llm-vcr](https://github.com/SiluPanda/llm-vcr), [llm-cassette](https://github.com/gangjig/llm-cassette), [llmvcr](https://pypi.org/project/llmvcr)), local-first eval harnesses as several more ([gatecheck](https://github.com/satchmakua/gatecheck)). Idea scarcity is not the binding constraint in this market; distribution, trust and maintenance are.

| Idea | Verdict | What overturns it |
|---|---|---|
| A — thin typed provider-agnostic layer | **Falsified as a product**, salvageable as a component | The differentiator already ships inside AI SDK 7 at 47M npm downloads/week |
| B — reliable Go/Rust retry/streaming helper | **Falsified as stated**, survives as failure semantics | Go AI adoption is shrinking, and retry is a 2015-era commodity |
| C — supply-chain-hardened library | **Falsified as a library**, reshaped into a local audit | Valid signatures and valid SLSA L3 attestations shipped on malicious releases |
| D — git-trackable prompt versioning | **Falsified** | The exact product exists free at 22 downloads/month; git is the product |
| E — lightweight "SQLAlchemy-level" helper | **Falsified** | It is A with a metaphor attached; the layer is commoditized |

For A, the claim was that a thin typed layer exposing provider metadata would win because abstractions hide token usage and provider-specific parameters. The demand is real and old — developers could not reach token-usage metadata through existing abstractions ([HN, Why we no longer use LangChain](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=40739982)) — but the supply side has since closed it. Vercel shipped **AI SDK 7 on 25 June 2026** with a standardized `reasoning` option that maps to provider-native settings, typed escape hatches via `providerOptions: { anthropic: {...} } satisfies AnthropicLanguageModelOptions`, per-step `responseTimeMs`/`outputTokensPerSecond`/`timeToFirstOutputMs` statistics, and lifecycle callbacks exposing `usage` and `finishReason` ([Vercel AI SDK 7](https://vercel.com/blog/ai-sdk-7)). That is precisely A's proposed differentiator, already running at **47,150,887 npm downloads in the week of 11–17 November 2026** ([npm registry](https://api.npmjs.org/downloads/point/last-week/ai)). A new entrant would reimplement an escape hatch with fewer providers.

The Python half is not empty either, and the shape of its occupancy is the argument. LiteLLM holds **63,518 stars and 99,782,353 downloads in 30 days**, Pydantic AI **6,964,997/month**, Instructor **10,244,752/month**, BAML **527,645/month** ([pypistats: litellm](https://pypistats.org/packages/litellm), [pydantic-ai](https://pypistats.org/packages/pydantic-ai), [instructor](https://pypistats.org/packages/instructor), [baml-py](https://pypistats.org/packages/baml-py)). The revealing data point is Mirascope, which brands itself "The LLM Anti-Framework" and sits at **1,535 stars and 20,239 downloads/month** — a ~5,000x gap to the "bloated" incumbent it opposes ([Mirascope](https://github.com/Mirascope/mirascope)). Thinness wins arguments, not installs. Meanwhile the original metadata pitch is moot in a second sense: `models.dev`, the provider-metadata dataset, is itself a **7,686-star open project** ([models.dev](https://github.com/models.dev)), so a metadata-exposing client adds types and drift-checking, not information.

Idea E is the same idea wearing a metaphor. The "SQLAlchemy level" framing comes from a single January 2025 HN comment distinguishing thin bridges from "Django-level" frameworks ([HN comment, jackmpcollins](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42803483)), and the author's own project built to that thesis, magentic, sits at **2,429 stars after roughly two years** ([magentic](https://github.com/jackmpcollins/magentic)). Every occupant of the low-level bridge — LiteLLM, aisuite at 16,404 stars, Simon Willison's `llm` at 12,779 — already does the job for free, and developer sentiment points past libraries entirely for simple cases: "I'd have to spend a whole lot more time learning how to use them than if I just imported the OpenAI Python library directly" ([HN, Simon Willison](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=36645575)). There is no separate demand signal for "SQLAlchemy-level" distinct from "thin typed layer," because the market already voted on that shape and the vote was for the incumbent.

One premise from the earlier landscape pass has to be retired explicitly: that developers "decisively moved" to modular single-responsibility helpers. The aesthetic preference is documented, the adoption is not. The thin tools measured here hold **1.5k–2.4k stars** while the bundled frameworks hold **9k–63k**, and `langchain` still records **157,123,296 downloads/month**, which does not support a collapse narrative even after discounting transitive pulls ([pypistats: langchain](https://pypistats.org/packages/langchain)). Thin helpers are a taste, and tastes do not ship roadmaps.

## Go and Rust look empty because the buyers are absent

The Go/Rust vacuum is real and stated by a competitor: "There is no Go equivalent of the Vercel AI SDK and that gap is not one framework's to fill," written by the maintainer of a Go LLM library who also noted langchaingo "had not been pushed in seven months" ([pkg.go.dev: tjo/llm](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/jimmitjoo/tjo/llm)). The category ceiling is also proven: `sashabaranov/go-openai` holds **10,812 stars against 230 open issues**, and `tmc/langchaingo` holds **9,784 stars against 435 open issues** ([go-openai](https://github.com/sashabaranov/go-openai), [langchaingo](https://github.com/tmc/langchaingo)). The problem is that the ceiling belongs to single-provider clients and general frameworks, not to a new thin helper, and the population underneath them is shrinking rather than underserved.

The Go team's own first-party data overturns the demand case. **78% of respondents are not building AI-powered features**, 66% report no AI functionality at all, and only **11%** work with "ML models, tools, or agents" — down 14 points from 2024's 59% uninvolved/39% involved split ([Go Developer Survey 2025](https://go.dev/blog/survey2025)). The widely repeated "22% of Go developers build AI features" figure inside library READMEs is a misreading of the same survey, which separates 20% "includes AI features I developed myself" from 12% "didn't develop AI features" ([Gigazine summary](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260123-go-developer-survey-2025)). A vacuum in an ecosystem where four in five developers are not doing the work is not an opportunity; it is the explanation for the vacuum.

The competitive evidence points the same way. Every differentiator a new entrant would propose is already claimed by zero-star projects: `elelem` advertises retries that "stop the instant output starts streaming" and a ledger of failed-attempt cost, `langrails` advertises zero dependencies and `Retry-After` handling, `looper-agent` advertises circuit breakers ([elelem](https://github.com/psyb0t/elelem), [langrails](https://github.com/promptrails/langrails/), [looper-agent](https://github.com/cuatroochenta-idi/looper-agent)). Instructor's official Go port sits at **223 stars and an untagged pseudo-version** ([instructor-go](https://github.com/instructor-ai/instructor-go)), Rust's direct Instructor analogue `rstructor` at **5,436 downloads in the last 90 days** ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/rstructor)), and the dominant Rust option is a framework, not a helper — `rig-core` at **4.54M total downloads** ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/rig-core)). Retry specifically is a solved commodity: `hashicorp/go-retryablehttp` holds **2,363 stars and has been conceptually untouched since 2015** ([repo](https://github.com/hashicorp/go-retryablehttp)).

What survives is the part Go developers genuinely cannot write in an afternoon, and it is not retry. It is SSE framing, resumable streams, idempotency keys, token accounting on aborted streams, and — most sharply — the refusal to emit half-parsed tool-call JSON that a caller cannot act on, a rationale stated by a competing maintainer: "a half-parsed JSON argument list is not something a caller can act on" ([pkg.go.dev](https://github.com/jimmitjoo/tjo/llm)). Note also that the reliability evidence itself is Python-shaped: the bug record comes from Instructor, where a streaming-plus-retry crash labelled `priority:critical` was **fixed and released the next day as v1.14.3** ([issue #1991](https://github.com/567-labs/instructor/issues/1991), [release](https://github.com/567-labs/instructor/releases/tag/v1.14.3)), and the backlog is 29 open issues against 10.2M monthly downloads. "The reference implementation is buggy" is a decaying argument, and it was never measured in Go or Rust at all.

## Prompt versioning lost to a directory and a diff

Idea D has the most articulated pain and the least revealed demand, which is usually a signal that the job is already being done without a dependency. A production write-up documents the exact failure the product would prevent — classification accuracy falling from **~88% to 71%** with no way to reconstruct which prompt version was running when quality was good — and the author's chosen fix was a `/prompts` directory in git plus an eval gate ([mohakdeepsingh.dev](https://mohakdeepsingh.dev/blog/how-i-actually-version-prompts-production-not-ideal-system)). The same account explains why a prompt database lost: renaming `{{contract_text}}` to `{{document_text}}` broke at runtime with a KeyError, whereas "with git, that kind of change shows up in the same diff as the code change." The canonical expert recommendation is identical: store prompts in git and treat them as ordinary software artifacts ([Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-should-i-version-and-manage-prompts.html)).

The near-controlled experiment is that the exact proposed product already exists, free, and has no users. `promptfile` ships YAML prompt files, render, diff, validate, a registry, a CLI and a CI recipe, under the tagline "No servers. No SaaS. No database." — and holds **0 stars, 1 commit and 22 PyPI downloads per month** ([promptfile](https://github.com/maheshmakvana/promptfiles), [pypistats](https://pypistats.org/packages/promptfiles)). A second git-native attempt, PromptOps, records **177 downloads/month** ([pypistats](https://pypistats.org/packages/llmhq-promptops)); `graver`, marketing itself as "zero dependencies, works offline," has **2 stars** ([graver](https://github.com/PracticalMind/graver)); and a better-resourced prompt registry launched on HN to **3 points** and the founder's own report of "pretty much no users so far" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49232345)). When the feature set is fully implemented three times over and adoption is zero, the feature set is not the bottleneck.

The durable value in this space sits where the proposed library is not: the gate and the audit trail. That is owned — promptfoo holds **27,438 stars and 4.83M npm downloads/month** for evals-as-code ([promptfoo](https://github.com/promptfoo/promptfoo), [npm](https://api.npmjs.org/downloads/point/last-month/promptfoo)) — and runtime deployment state is owned by platforms, with Langfuse at **37,613 stars** ([Langfuse](https://github.com/langfuse/langfuse)) and every 2026 roundup listing LangWatch, PromptLayer, LangSmith and Braintrust rather than git-native libraries ([LangWatch](https://langwatch.ai/blog/best-prompt-management-tools)). The one thing git cannot do is the one thing a git-only tool cannot sell: non-engineer editing and deployed-state visibility ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/why-your-prompts-dont-belong-in-git/)). Teams also report git-only prompt workflows collapsing past a handful of templates with "version conflicts everywhere" ([Latenode community](https://community.latenode.com/t/managing-prompt-templates-in-git-repositories-with-langsmith-any-success-stories/39403)).

## Signing was present and useless, so the opening is recomputable evidence

Idea C was the only candidate with a forced budget line rather than a developer preference, and the 2026 record explains both why it matters and why its stated mechanism fails. LiteLLM was compromised **twice in one year**: in March, versions 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 shipped a `litellm_init.pth` file — a Python startup hook that runs on interpreter launch with no user interaction — harvesting SSH keys, `.env` files, cloud credentials and kubeconfigs, then creating privileged pods in `kube-system` ([GHSA-92x9-889m-jgmw](https://github.com/advisories/GHSA-92x9-889m-jgmw), [pydevtools](https://pydevtools.com/blog/litellm-supply-chain-attack-and-securing-python-dependencies/)); in October, **seven malicious 1.107.0 wheels sat live on PyPI between 11:39 and 13:23 UTC** plus four Docker tags including `latest` ([LiteLLM incident report](https://docs.litellm.ai/blog/security-incident-oct-21-2026)). Exposure is estimated at **2,500+ organisations and 434,000 CI/CD pipelines** ([CloudSEK](https://www.cloudsek.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-breach-2500-companies-434000-cicd-pipelines), [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/litellm-breach-data-shows-supply-chain-attack-impacted-2488-firms/)), Mercor disclosed it was hit ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/31/mercor-says-it-was-hit-by-cyberattack-tied-to-compromise-of-open-source-litellm-project/)), and the March "Tell HN" post reached **938 points** ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47501426)).

The claim that dies is "we are the hardened, signed library." LiteLLM had already moved to Trusted Publishers, immutable tags and cosign signing after March, and the October attacker defeated all of it with three stolen GitHub CLI OAuth tokens — one belonging to an org owner — to disable branch protection, force-push a workflow change, and point the releaser at the lookalike repo `Berri-AI/litellm` ([LiteLLM incident report](https://docs.litellm.ai/blog/security-incident-oct-21-2026)). **The compromised v1.107.0 images carried a valid signature from the previous key.** The same failure repeated at ecosystem scale: **633 malicious npm packages cleared Sigstore provenance checks** using stolen credentials ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/security/npm-sigstore-provenance-stolen-identity-audit-grid-2026)), and the May 2026 "Mini Shai-Hulud" worm compromised **169 npm and 2 PyPI packages — 373 malicious package-version entries** including `mistralai 2.4.6` and `guardrails-ai 0.10.1` — with **valid SLSA Build Level 3 provenance attestations** ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-mini-shai-hulud-ai-supply-chain-20260513-c), [CSA on provenance](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-mini-shai-hulud-ai-supply-chain)). Provenance penetration is also too thin to lean on: **7.2%** of Sigstore's own npm dependency tree carries attestations ([Zenn](https://zenn.dev/sqer/articles/e4df3d397f5651?locale=en)).

The second falsifier is subtler and more damaging to the business case: the market absorbed the demand without a new library. PyPI now rejects new files on releases older than 14 days, `uv` ships `uv audit` plus an install-time malware check, pip v26+ adds `--uploaded-prior-to`, and seven package managers converged on release-age cooldowns with Dependabot defaulting to **3 days** and pnpm to **1 day** ([pydevtools](https://pydevtools.com/handbook/explanation/what-is-a-dependency-cooldown/), [PyPI incident report](https://blog.pypi.org/posts/2026-04-02-incident-report-litellm-telnyx-supply-chain-attack/)). Enterprise buyers were protected not by a better OSS dependency but by a **private enterprise release** ([LiteLLM incident report](https://docs.litellm.ai/blog/security-incident-oct-21-2026)), and the generic scanner layer is occupied by account-gated tools — Snyk's `agent-scan` needs a token and sends data to Snyk's API ([31-tool survey](https://pournasserian.com/writing/mcp-server-testing-tools-2026)). Even the punishment signal is absent: LiteLLM's measured **99.8M downloads/month** shows no visible collapse after two compromises.

What survives is the inversion: an auditor asserts nothing about itself, it reports on other people's packages, and the user recomputes the evidence locally so a stolen credential cannot forge the verdict. That is also the only defensible answer to "you are now trusting one more maintainer." The AI-specific surface makes it non-generic: agents install code at runtime from MCP configs, skills and plugin manifests across ~**94,600 MCP servers** with ~500M monthly SDK downloads ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)), and generic SCA does not model install-time hooks, base-URL overrides or capability acquisition between versions. Independent corroboration that dependency trust is a named, measured pain: **26% of Go developers** rank "finding trustworthy Go modules and packages" a top-3 frustration ([Go Developer Survey 2025](https://go.dev/blog/survey2025)).

### Why the ranked three fall in this order

Scoring below is judgment, 1–5 per criterion, on the evidence above; "gap" means defensible space against named incumbents, not absence of competitors. The inversion is the finding: the two ideas with the best-documented abstraction pain rank last, because abstraction pain is the most contested and least monetisable pain in the market.

| # | Idea | Demand | Gap | Feasibility | Differentiation | 24-mo durability | Total /25 | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **C′** — local-first AI/agent dependency audit (+ minimal-surface client later) | 5 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 4 | **22** | Consumed as a post-incident one-off, never wired into CI |
| 2 | **F** — MCP readiness audit, fixes proven by re-scoring, open host rule pack | 5 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 | **21** | Audience is hobbyist server authors who don't adopt audit tooling |
| 3 | **B′** — Go/Rust client sold on failure semantics | 2 | 3 | 5 | 3 | 4 | **17** | TAM too small to sustain even 200–600 stars/month |

Rank 2, the MCP readiness audit, has the highest absolute demand and the cleanest incumbent abstention. The official Inspector's roadmap states **"no LLM or eval work planned"** and "the Inspector is not a host," with deterministic assertions and conformance arriving between November 2026 and January 2027 — meaning the testing layer will be eaten while the judge, design and host-audit layer is deliberately left alone ([Inspector roadmap 2026-H2](https://github.com/modelcontextprotocol/inspector/blob/main/docs/inspector-roadmap-2026-h2.md)). The specific open cells — local-first AI evaluation, "fixes, not just findings," and an open multi-host rule pack covering Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor, VS Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, Kiro and Windsurf together — are rated high openness with no open tool covering them ([31-tool survey](https://pournasserian.com/writing/mcp-server-testing-tools-2026)). Demand at scale is not in doubt: **97.1% of 856 tool descriptions contain at least one defect** ([arXiv 2602.14878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14878)). The variance is what holds it to second: its value depends on a rule pack becoming a de facto standard, the protocol was redesigned statelessly in July 2026 with five revisions in twenty months, and the paying audience — companies submitting to the ChatGPT store or Claude directory — is small and already served by closed products such as Alpic Beacon and Manufact.

Rank 3 survives only in its reframed form, and the honest reading of its evidence is that it is a dependency play, not a business. The persuasive wedge is a published conformance matrix across streaming, validation failure, retry, cancellation and cache-key isolation, with the helper as the only implementation that passes — the same move that makes C′ credible, applied to behaviour instead of dependencies. One language first, a roughly 300-line core, test vectors shared with a later Rust port. Success looks like low thousands of stars, not revenue, and the realistic baseline is the observed **200–600 new stars/month** convergence for post-launch developer tools ([Nine Agent Frameworks](https://blog.mikegchambers.com/posts/agentic-framework-landscape/)).

Two epistemic caveats belong in the ranking itself. The MCP gap analysis rests substantially on **one detailed secondary source** — a single author's 31-tool comparison dated 1 October 2026 — so its openness ratings are one expert's assessment, not a consensus. And no source found quantifies how many teams actually changed dependency policy after the LiteLLM incidents; the security demand case is incident-based and budget-inferred, not transaction-observed. Both caveats argue for the cheapest possible test rather than more analysis.

## The MVP is a four-second replay of a real breach

The strongest idea should be built as `npx depaudit` (equally `uvx` or `go install`): zero config, zero account, zero telemetry, fully functional offline after one ruleset fetch. Six weeks, one or two people. The first three weeks produce only the report. Inputs are lockfiles across ecosystems — `package-lock.json`, `pnpm-lock.yaml`, `uv.lock`, `poetry.lock`, `go.sum`, `Cargo.lock` — plus agent surfaces such as `.mcp.json`, `claude_desktop_config.json`, `.cursor/mcp.json` and skill or plugin directories. Findings map to a **capability, never a CVE**: install-time code execution via npm `postinstall`/`preinstall`, wheel build hooks and `setup.py`; network egress at build or import; obfuscated or packed source; native binaries fetched after install; unpinned ranges that resolve at install time; telemetry on by default; and base-URL override, the exact vector that turns a "gateway" into an exfiltration path. Output is a human report plus **SARIF** plus a non-zero exit code, with `--diff <ref>` to gate only what a pull request adds. The demo that sells the tool is one command: `depaudit diff litellm@1.106.x..1.107.0` failing in under five seconds with the finding that the release adds an import-time hook reading `~/.aws` and posting to an external host.

Weeks four to six build the asset rather than the surface. The rule pack becomes versioned data — `rules/*.yaml`, each rule carrying its rationale and a source URL, semver'd and signed, pinnable by the user — because that pack, not the scanner code, is what compounds and what generic SCA cannot cheaply replicate for AI-specific surfaces. Evidence receipts hash the analysed tree plus the ruleset version into a signed artifact a team can attach to a release, so a reviewer who re-runs gets the same verdict; that is the direct, honest answer to "provenance was valid but the package was malicious." The default output for any version bump becomes a **capability delta** — what did this release newly acquire — deliberately out of the CVE business.

The minimal-surface client belongs at v0.3 and only if the gates are met, because shipping it first repeats idea A's mistake: it would enter a comparison with a 47M-downloads-per-week incumbent instead of with nothing that is both local and AI-aware. When it does ship, it is a single-dependency or stdlib-only client for three providers plus a `hardened(sdk)` wrapper for teams keeping their existing SDK — pinning base URL, disabling telemetry, forbidding env-var-driven redirects, refusing to run when a lockfile hash drifts.

The gates are ordered and cheap: a stranger runs the tool on their own repository within two weeks of the demo post; at least **20% of runs use `--diff`**, which proves a CI habit rather than curiosity; and one external team pins a ruleset version, which proves the pack is the product. Explicit non-goals are the vulnerability database, runtime sandboxing, prompt-injection scanning of tool descriptions (Snyk and Cisco own it), LLM-judge evals, and any hosted dashboard or account. If gate three fails, the fallback is not "build the client anyway" but to sell the rule pack to platforms — which is how this category's funded players actually monetise.

## Conclusion

The useful shift in understanding is that "unmet need plus no incumbent" has stopped being a signal in this market. Every gap probed already has several zero-traction attempts, so the discriminating conditions are narrower: an incumbent that has *publicly declined* the territory, or a substitute that is *structurally unable* to work. The Inspector roadmap declining eval and host-audit work, and signatures shipping on malicious artifacts, are the two clean cases — and they are the only two that produced a defensible position. Everywhere else, the true complaint was real, which is exactly why the space fills with well-intentioned clones.

A second implication deserves stating plainly, because it contradicts the natural reading of the sentiment data: in late 2026 the thing that decides whether a developer tool compounds is not whether developers agree with its argument but whether its output is a habit. Thinness, transparency and better ergonomics are all arguments. A `--diff` gate that fails a pull request is a habit. That is why a dependency auditor outranks a provider abstraction by nine points on the same scale, and why the six-week test is deliberately designed to fail fast on adoption rather than on engineering.
